Simple Linear Regression

Welcome back. In our last session, we talked extensively about functions. We learned that a function is simply a mechanism—a recipe that maps an input to an output.

Today, we are going to use that exact mathematical foundation to solve our very first machine learning problem from scratch: Simple Linear Regression.

[PAUSE: Let the scatter plot animation begin drawing points on screen]

Let's set the stage. Look at the screen. Imagine we have a scattering of data points on an X-Y plane. Each point represents a piece of historical data.

Let's make this concrete. Let's say X is the square footage of a house, and Y is the price that house sold for. When we look at these dots, human beings are incredibly good at spotting visual trends.

Take a look at it. You can probably see immediately that as the square footage goes up, the price goes up. The points move in a roughly linear direction from the bottom left to the top right.

Our fundamental goal in machine learning is to get the computer to see what we see. We want to draw a single straight line through this data that best captures that underlying trend. If we can find that mathematically perfect line, we can use it to predict the price of a house that isn't even on this graph yet.

The Knobs: W zero and W one

So, how do we draw a line mathematically? Let's recall from our last lecture the formula for a degree-one polynomial.

Y hat equals W zero, plus W one times X.

Notice I called it "Y hat". In machine learning, we use a little hat over the Y to remind ourselves that this is our model's prediction, not the actual true data point.

Remember that the X is our incoming data—the house size. The W zero and W one are our parameters. I want you to think of them as physical knobs on our function machine.

[PAUSE: Animation shows a random line appearing, and a hand twisting knobs to move it]

If we turn the W one knob, we change the slope of the line. Watch how the line gets steeper, or flatter, or even points downward. It represents how much the price increases for every one unit increase in square footage.

If we turn the W zero knob, we change the intercept. Watch how the entire line slides straight up or straight down without changing its angle. This is our baseline value.

Now, I could walk up to this machine, close my eyes, and just twist those two knobs randomly. A line will appear on the screen. But it will probably be a terrible line. It might slice right through empty space, nowhere near our data points.

So, the critical question we have to answer today is this: How do we mathematically define what a good line is? How do we find the absolute best knobs to turn to?

Quantifying "Goodness": The Error

To find the best line, we first have to figure out how to measure how bad a line is.

[PAUSE: Animation pauses on one specific data point and draws a vertical dashed line down to the solid prediction line]

Let's put a random line on the screen. Pick just one data point. Look at the vertical distance between that actual historical point, and where our line predicts that point should be.

That vertical distance is our error. We sometimes call this the residual. It is simply the actual Y, minus our predicted Y-hat.

If the dot is exactly on the line, the error is zero. We made a perfect prediction. If the dot is far above the line, the error is a large positive number. If the dot is below the line, the error is a negative number.

Now, we want a line that is good for the entire dataset, not just one point. So, a logical first thought might be: let's just add all these errors up! Let's sum up (Y minus Y-hat) for every point.

But there is a mathematical trap there.

[PAUSE: Animation highlights one point +10 units above, and one point -10 units below]

Suppose we have a point that is exactly 10 units above the line. And another point that is exactly 10 units below the line. Those errors are positive 10 and negative 10. If we add them together, they cancel out to zero!

The computer would look at that sum and think our line is absolutely perfect, even though it is totally missing both points.

To fix this cancellation problem, we do something very simple but incredibly powerful: we square the errors.

The Loss Function: Mean Squared Error

When you square a negative number, it becomes positive. Negative 10 squared is 100. Positive 10 squared is also 100. So by squaring all of our errors, they stop canceling each other out. Every error, whether above or below the line, adds to the total.

A secondary, very important benefit is that squaring heavily penalizes really bad predictions. An error of 2 becomes a penalty of 4. But an error of 10 becomes a massive penalty of 100. Our model will be highly motivated to avoid huge outliers.

So, we square the errors for every single point in our dataset, and we add them all up using our summation sigma. Then, to keep things consistent regardless of dataset size, we divide by N—the total number of points—to get the average.

[PAUSE: Animation reveals the full MSE formula on screen]

This gives us a single, highly important metric: the Mean Squared Error, or MSE.

In machine learning, we call this a Loss Function or a Cost Function. It is a single number that tells us exactly how terrible our current line is. The higher the MSE, the worse our line fits the data.

Our goal, therefore, is optimization. We want to minimize this loss. We want to find the exact combination of W zero and W one that makes this Mean Squared Error as small as mathematically possible.

Finding the Minimum: Enter Calculus

In our last lecture, when we talked about polynomial families, I told you to keep the shape of a quadratic function in mind—that U-shaped bowl.

Well, look at our MSE formula. We squared our errors. Our parameters, W zero and W one, are inside that square. Because it is a squared function, if we were to graph our loss for every possible combination of W zero and W one, it creates a three-dimensional surface that looks exactly like a bowl.

[PAUSE: Animation shows a 3D bowl surface, representing the loss landscape]

The very bottom of that bowl represents the lowest possible error. The coordinates at the bottom of that bowl are our perfect W zero and W one. So how do we find the bottom?

This is where Calculus comes to the rescue. Specifically, we use derivatives.

Without getting bogged down in complex textbook definitions, I want you to remember one thing: a derivative simply measures the slope or the steepness of a curve at any given point.

If you drop a marble into a bowl, it rolls down the steep sides. But right at the very bottom of the bowl, the surface becomes perfectly flat. The tangent line is horizontal. The slope is exactly zero.

So, the mathematical trick to finding the minimum of our loss function is brilliantly simple: we take the derivative of our loss function, and we set it equal to zero.

The Partial Derivatives

Now, because our line has two knobs—W zero and W one—our loss bowl is three-dimensional. We can't just take one derivative. We have to take two.

We need to find how the loss changes when we tweak W zero, and how the loss changes when we tweak W one.

We take what is called a partial derivative of the Loss with respect to W zero. Then we take the partial derivative of the Loss with respect to W one.

We set both of those equations to zero. What we are doing is mathematically demanding that the computer find the exact point where the bowl is flat in both the North-South direction, and the East-West direction.

[PAUSE: Animation puts the two partial derivative equations on screen equal to 0]

The Algebra: Solving for the Knobs

Once we take those derivatives and set them to zero, the calculus is over! From here on out, it is purely high school algebra. We have a system of two equations, and two unknowns.

Let's look at the first equation, the derivative with respect to W zero.

By applying the chain rule, the derivative of our squared error brings a 2 down to the front. We set it to zero, which allows us to divide out the 2 and the 1 over N. They disappear.

We are left with the sum of (Y minus W zero, minus W one times X) equals zero.

[PAUSE: Talk through the animation as it expands the summation]

Let's distribute that summation across our terms. We get the sum of Y, minus the sum of W zero, minus W one times the sum of X, all equal to zero.

Now, think about this: if we sum up a constant W zero, N times, that is just N times W zero. Let's substitute that in.

Next, let's divide the entire equation by N. This is where it gets elegant. The sum of Y divided by N is simply the mean of Y, which we call Y-bar. The sum of X divided by N is the mean of X, which we call X-bar. And the N attached to our W zero cancels out.

If we rearrange this, moving W zero to the other side, we get our first solution:

W zero equals Y-bar, minus W one times X-bar.

This is a profound mathematical statement. It tells us that our perfect line must pass exactly through the average of all X's and the average of all Y's. If you know the center of mass of your data, and you know your slope W one, you instantly know your intercept.

[PAUSE: Let audience digest the W0 solution. Animation shifts to the W1 equation]

But we still need to find W one. To do that, we take our second equation—the derivative with respect to W one.

This derivative looks similar, but because of the chain rule on the inner function, an extra X pops out at the end.

We set it to zero. We expand the terms just like before, multiplying the X through. We get the sum of (X times Y), minus W zero times the sum of X, minus W one times the sum of X squared, equals zero.

Now, we take that beautiful formula for W zero we just found, and we substitute it right into this equation.

[PAUSE: Animation shows the substitution and algebraic rearranging. This takes time.]

This part requires a few lines of algebraic factoring—grouping all the terms with W one on one side, and moving everything else to the other side.

When we factor out W one and divide to isolate it, the dust finally settles. We arrive at what we call the Closed Form Solution.

We get a direct, explicit formula for W one. It is the covariance of X and Y, divided by the variance of X.

Think about what this means. We don't have to guess. We don't have to turn the knobs randomly and check the error. We don't need a loop.

Because we used calculus to find the bottom of the loss bowl, we now have a magic formula. You hand this formula your historical X and Y data, you calculate the sums and means, and out pops the mathematically perfect slope and intercept. The absolute best line of fit.

This is the power of combining our intuitive understanding of functions with the optimization of calculus. You have just looked under the hood of your first true machine learning algorithm.
